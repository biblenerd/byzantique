---
anchor: ROM 1:7
title: Grace to you and peace
---

>"Grace and peace to you" is a remarkable combination of a Greek salutation *charis* (grace) and the ancient Hebrew blessing *shalom* (peace).... In this unique greeting, Paul addresses Gentile and Jewish believers together, as members of one church....
>
>Notice that Paul does not write, "*Charis* to you Greeks and *shalom* to you Hebrews." Grace is not just for Gentiles and peace is not just for Jews. God desires the whole body of Christ to receive his grace and to experience his peace. Paul writes with respect for his readers' own ethnic and cultural backgrounds, yet he points to a new countercultural reality&mdash;a community in which the barriers between Jews and Gentiles is broken down and eliminated.[^fn1]

This is St. Paul's common greeting in his epistles. Other instances of this greeting include [1 Corinthians 1:3](ref:1CO 1:3); [2 Corinthians 1:2](ref:2CO 1:2); [Galatians 1:3](ref:GAL 1:3); [Ephesians 1:2](ref:EPH 1:2); [Philippians 1:2](ref:PHP 1:2); [Colossians 1:2](ref:COL 1:2); [1 Thessalonians 1:1](ref:1TH 1:1); [2 Thessalonians 1:2](ref:2TH 1:2);  [Philemon 1:3](ref:PHM 1:3).

Another variation is "grace, mercy, and peace" which is seen in the Pastoral Epistles ([1 Timothy 1:2](ref:1TI 1:2); [2 Timothy 1:2](ref:2TI 1:2); [Titus 1:4](ref:TIT 1:4)) and also in [2 John 1:3](ref:2JN 1:3).

Other authors also use variations of this greeting (e.g., [1 Peter 1:2](ref:1PE 1:2); [2 Peter 1:2](ref:2PE 1:2); [Revelation 1:4](ref:REV 1:4)). The author of Jude departed from using grace / *charis* ([Jude 1:2](ref:JUD 1:2) &mdash; "... mercy, peace, and love ...").

[^fn1]: Stephen J. Binz, *Panorama of the Bible: New Testament* (Collegeville, MN: Liturgical Press, 2016), 65.
